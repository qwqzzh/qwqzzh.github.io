PART 1

Fun with Filters

PART 1.1

Convolutions from Scratch!

For an image I and kernel K, 2D convolution computes (I ∗ K)[y,x] = ΣiΣjI[y−i,x−j]K[i,j]. In code, I first flip the kernel in both axes and slide it across the image. I implemented this operation using only NumPy in two ways: a literal four-loop implementation and a two-loop implementation that replaces the two inner loops with an element-wise product and np.sum.

Both functions support rectangular kernels and return an output with the same shape as the input. For a kh × kw kernel, I pad the image by half the kernel size before sliding. Pixels outside the original image are filled with zero, matching SciPy's boundary="fill", fillvalue=0. This choice is simple and makes the comparison exact, although filters near the border see artificial black pixels and can therefore produce darker blur values or strong derivative responses.

MY PHOTOGRAPH

Four explicit loops

def convolve2d_four_loops(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    padded = np.pad(
        image,
        ((kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2),
         (kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2)),
        mode="constant"
    )
    flipped = np.flip(kernel, axis=(0, 1))
    output = np.zeros_like(image, dtype=float)

    for y in range(image.shape[0]):
        for x in range(image.shape[1]):
            for ky in range(kh):
                for kx in range(kw):
                    output[y, x] += (
                        padded[y + ky, x + kx]
                        * flipped[ky, kx]
                    )
    return output

Two loops + vectorized window

def convolve2d_two_loops(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    padded = np.pad(
        image,
        ((kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2),
         (kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2)),
        mode="constant"
    )
    flipped = np.flip(kernel, axis=(0, 1))
    output = np.zeros_like(image, dtype=float)

    for y in range(image.shape[0]):
        for x in range(image.shape[1]):
            window = padded[y:y+kh, x:x+kw]
            output[y, x] = np.sum(window * flipped)
    return output

SciPy baseline

scipy_result = convolve2d(
    image, kernel, mode="same",
    boundary="fill", fillvalue=0

Comparison and analysis

I applied all three implementations to the 800 × 692 grayscale portrait using a normalized 9 × 9 box filter. The four-loop version took 10.870 s, the two-loop version took 1.376 s, and SciPy took 0.039 s. Replacing the inner Python loops with NumPy made my implementation about 7.9× faster; SciPy was another 35× faster because the entire convolution runs in optimized library code. The maximum differences from SciPy were only 2.44 × 10−15 and 7.77 × 10−16; Dx and Dy matched exactly. These floating-point-scale errors verify that the kernel flip, indexing, padding, and output shape are correct.

PART 1.2

Finite Difference Operator

The finite-difference filters Dx = [1, −1] and Dy = [1, −1]T approximate the first partial derivatives of image intensity. A large response to Dx means neighboring pixels change strongly along the horizontal direction, so it highlights predominantly vertical edges; Dy analogously highlights horizontal edges. Because the responses are signed, opposite brightness transitions appear with opposite signs.

I combine the directional derivatives into the nonnegative gradient magnitude ‖∇I‖ = √(Ix2 + Iy2). This measures edge strength independently of orientation. Binarization then classifies a pixel as an edge when its magnitude exceeds a chosen threshold.

I tested thresholds 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25, then selected 0.20. Lower values preserve more weak texture but admit noise in the grass and background; higher values begin to remove real contours from the camera, tripod, and silhouette. The selected value is a qualitative compromise.

PART 1.3

Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) Filter

Finite differences amplify rapid pixel-to-pixel variation, so they respond not only to object boundaries but also to sensor noise and fine texture. I first convolved the cameraman image with a normalized 9 × 9 Gaussian kernel with σ = 2. The Gaussian is a low-pass filter: nearby values are averaged with smoothly decreasing weights, attenuating high-frequency noise before differentiation.

Comparison with Part 1.2: the unsmoothed finite-difference magnitude is thin but visibly noisy, especially in the grass and background. After Gaussian smoothing, isolated responses disappear and the important contours of the person, camera, and tripod become more continuous. The remaining edges are slightly wider and less precisely localized because smoothing spreads an intensity transition across neighboring pixels. The smoothed result therefore trades a small amount of localization for substantially better noise suppression; this also allows a lower threshold of 0.08.

Finally, I formed DoG kernels by convolving G with Dx and Dy. Associativity gives (I ∗ G) ∗ D = I ∗ (G ∗ D), so the two-stage procedure and a single convolution with each precomputed DoG filter should agree. Their maximum interior magnitude difference was 6.80 × 10−16, confirming that the two approaches are numerically equivalent apart from boundary effects and floating-point rounding.

PART 2.1

Image “Sharpening”

Isharp = I + α(I − Gσ ∗ I)

unsharp masking = original + scaled high frequencies

A Gaussian blur retains low frequencies; subtracting it from the original isolates high frequencies. Adding those details back with amount α increases local edge contrast. I combined the operations into one convolution kernel, (1 + α)δ − αG, and used a 9 × 9 Gaussian with σ = 2.

On the Taj Mahal, increasing α makes edges crisper but eventually creates halos and harsh texture. The blur-then-sharpen test is more revealing: sharpening restores contrast around surviving edges, yet it cannot reconstruct frequencies actually removed by the blur. That is why the recovered portrait looks clearer but not identical to the original.

Choosing α by the kind of blur.

The sightseeing photograph is slightly out of focus; α = 1.5 makes the columns and subject outline noticeably clearer without becoming too harsh. Both photographs become noticeably clearer while still looking natural, and the method remains simple enough that every change can be explained through frequency components.

PART 2.2

Hybrid Images

High frequencies dominate at close range, while only smooth low frequencies remain visible at a distance. A hybrid image adds a Gaussian low-pass of one source to a complementary high-pass of another:

H = GσL ∗ A + w[B − GσH ∗ B]

FAVORITE RESULT · COMPLETE PROCESS

The rock that grows a cat face.

MY CREATIVE EXPLORATION

I noticed that the central seaside rock already resembles a seated animal. The strongest reversed hybrid uses the rock as the low-frequency image and a horizontally flipped frontal tabby as the high-frequency image. I aligned the head-to-body axes and selected σL = 5, σH = 4, and cat weight w = 1.1. This slightly stronger high-frequency contribution makes the eyes, ears, whiskers, and fur clearer without overwhelming the rock silhouette. Reduced or viewed from afar, those details vanish and the seaside rock returns.

Color choice. I retained RGB for this final version because the sea-blue rock scene establishes a clear distant reading, while the tabby’s dark neutral fur remains legible as nearby high-frequency detail. Compared with grayscale, color gives the two viewing distances more atmosphere without obscuring the perceptual switch.

Two directions for a tree spirit.

With the elderly face low-passed (σ = 12) and bark high-passed (σ = 5), texture dominates nearby and the face emerges far away. Reversing the roles—tree σ = 4, face σ = 4, face weight 0.9—makes eyes and wrinkles visible nearby while the trunk takes over at a distance. Feathering the face boundary prevents its rectangular crop from becoming an artificial high-frequency edge.

Color choice. I kept the first ordering in grayscale because the face’s warm skin tone otherwise forms a conspicuous pink patch inside the pale bark, weakening the illusion. For the reversed ordering, RGB works better: the brown trunk supplies a coherent low-frequency color field, while the face contributes mostly fine structural detail. The surrounding green foliage also strengthens the reading of a tree spirit.

What “cutoff” means here

A Gaussian has a smooth response rather than a hard frequency boundary: H(f) = exp(−2π²σ²f²), with an approximate −3 dB cutoff fc = √ln2 / (2πσ) cycles per pixel. Increasing σ therefore lowers the effective cutoff and smooths more aggressively. The high-pass response is 1 − H(f). Every selected σ above came from visual comparison, not a universal preset.

PART 2.3

Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

A stack never downsamples. Starting from G0 = I, I repeatedly filter to form Gi+1 = G ∗ Gi. I used five levels and a 41 × 41 Gaussian with σ = 8, implemented without any built-in pyramid function.

The Laplacian levels are Li = Gi − Gi+1, with the final residual L4 = G4. Their sum telescopes back to the original image at floating-point precision.

BELLS & WHISTLES · COLOR

All final blends operate per RGB channel. Color is especially useful in the Oraple, Earth/Moon, sand, and lily experiments: coarse color transitions are smoothed by the mask stack while fine chromatic texture remains attached to its source. For the hybrid images, grayscale avoids conflicting color fields and makes the near/far frequency change easier to judge.

MOST IMPORTANT TAKEAWAY

Filtering is not merely “making an image blurry or sharp.” It decides which structures survive at a particular viewing scale.

The same Gaussian appears throughout the project, but its role changes: it suppresses noise before edge detection, defines the effective cutoff of a hybrid image, separates Laplacian bands, and turns a hard binary mask into a frequency-dependent seam. The most important practical lesson was that alignment and parameter choice matter as much as the equation. A mathematically valid blend can still fail perceptually when silhouettes, landmarks, colors, or frequency weights disagree.
