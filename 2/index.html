1.1 · CONVOLUTIONS FROM SCRATCH

Four loops, two loops, one operation.

I implemented same-size 2D convolution twice using NumPy: first with four explicit loops over image and kernel coordinates, then with two loops and a vectorized dot product over each local window. Both versions flip the kernel and apply zero padding, so they match scipy.signal.convolve2d(..., mode="same", boundary="fill").

On the 800 × 692 grayscale portrait and a 9 × 9 box filter, the four-loop version took 10.770 s; the two-loop version took 1.389 s. Their maximum errors against SciPy were 2.44 × 10−15 and 7.77 × 10−16. Zero padding preserves output dimensions but darkens border responses because out-of-frame pixels are treated as zero.

1.2 · FINITE DIFFERENCE OPERATOR

From derivatives to a binary edge map.

Convolving the cameraman with Dx = [1, −1] and Dy = [1, −1]T gives horizontal and vertical change. I combine them as ‖∇I‖ = √(Ix2 + Iy2) and threshold the magnitude.

I tested thresholds 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, and 0.25, then selected 0.20. Lower values preserve more weak texture but admit noise in the grass and background; higher values begin to remove real contours from the camera, tripod, and silhouette. The selected value is a qualitative compromise.

1.3 · DERIVATIVE OF GAUSSIAN

Smooth first; differentiate once.

A 9 × 9 Gaussian with σ = 2 suppresses high-frequency noise before differentiation. The resulting edges are thicker and more continuous than the raw finite-difference result, with much less texture in flat regions. I also convolved the Gaussian kernel with Dx and Dy to create derivative-of-Gaussian filters. By associativity, applying each DoG filter once should equal Gaussian smoothing followed by a finite difference; the measured interior difference is at floating-point precision.

PART 2.1

Image sharpening

Recovering presence,
not lost information.

Isharp = I + α(I − Gσ ∗ I)

unsharp masking = original + scaled high frequencies

A Gaussian blur retains low frequencies; subtracting it from the original isolates high frequencies. Adding those details back with amount α increases local edge contrast. I combined the operations into one convolution kernel, (1 + α)δ − αG, and used a 9 × 9 Gaussian with σ = 2.

On the Taj Mahal, increasing α makes edges crisper but eventually creates halos and harsh texture. The blur-then-sharpen test is more revealing: sharpening restores contrast around surviving edges, yet it cannot reconstruct frequencies actually removed by the blur. That is why the recovered portrait looks clearer but not identical to the original.

PERSONAL STUDY · TWO IMPERFECT MEMORIES

Choosing α by the kind of blur.

The sightseeing photograph is slightly out of focus; α = 1.5 makes the columns and subject outline noticeably clearer without becoming too harsh. The curtain-call photograph contains both motion blur and defocus, so α = 1.0 is safer: larger values mostly amplify hair, fabric, couch texture, and noise rather than recovering faces.

These results are satisfying but less refined than modern editing software, which can add edge-aware sharpening, denoising, lens correction, local masks, deblurring, or learned enhancement. This global filter is valuable precisely because its behavior remains simple and interpretable.

PART 2.2

Hybrid images

One image nearby.
Another from afar.

High frequencies dominate at close range, while only smooth low frequencies remain visible at a distance. A hybrid image adds a Gaussian low-pass of one source to a complementary high-pass of another:

H = GσL ∗ A + w[B − GσH ∗ B]

FAVORITE RESULT · COMPLETE PROCESS

The rock that grows a cat face.

MY CREATIVE EXPLORATION

I noticed that the central seaside rock already resembles a seated animal. The strongest reversed hybrid uses the rock as the low-frequency image and a horizontally flipped frontal tabby as the high-frequency image. I aligned the head-to-body axes and selected σL = 5, σH = 4, and cat weight w = 0.9. Nearby, symmetric eyes, ears, whiskers, and fur emerge; reduced or viewed from afar, those details vanish and the seaside rock returns.

SELF-PORTRAITS · CHANGE OF EXPRESSION

A frown that resolves into a smile.

Nearly identical pose, lighting, glasses, and background make the expression itself the dominant change. The selected direction uses the smile as low frequency and the frown as high frequency with (σL, σH, w) = (12, 5, 1.2). Furrowed brows and the downturned mouth lead nearby; the broad smile emerges as those details disappear.

EXTERNALLY SOURCED IMAGES · CREATIVE COMPOSITION

Two directions for a tree spirit.

With the elderly face low-passed (σ = 12) and bark high-passed (σ = 5), texture dominates nearby and the face emerges far away. Reversing the roles—tree σ = 4, face σ = 4, face weight 0.9—makes eyes and wrinkles visible nearby while the trunk takes over at a distance. Feathering the face boundary prevents its rectangular crop from becoming an artificial high-frequency edge.

What “cutoff” means here

A Gaussian has a smooth response rather than a hard frequency boundary: H(f) = exp(−2π²σ²f²), with an approximate −3 dB cutoff fc = √ln2 / (2πσ) cycles per pixel. Increasing σ therefore lowers the effective cutoff and smooths more aggressively. The high-pass response is 1 − H(f). Every selected σ above came from visual comparison, not a universal preset.

PART 2.3

Gaussian & Laplacian stacks

Same dimensions.
Different scales.

A stack never downsamples. Starting from G0 = I, I repeatedly filter to form Gi+1 = G ∗ Gi. I used five levels and a 41 × 41 Gaussian with σ = 8, implemented without any built-in pyramid function.

The Laplacian levels are Li = Gi − Gi+1, with the final residual L4 = G4. Their sum telescopes back to the original image at floating-point precision.

THE ORAPLE · VERTICAL STEP MASK

One hard decision, softened at every scale.

A binary mask selects apple on the left and orange on the right. Its Gaussian stack makes the transition increasingly broad at lower frequencies: fine fruit texture stays localized, while large illumination and color differences cross the seam gently. Compared with a direct cut, the five-level result hides the boundary without globally blurring either fruit.

CREATIVE COMPOSITION · SOURCED IMAGERY

An irregular mask follows a hand-drawn curve from the head through shoulder, torso, skirt, and lower edge. Low-frequency noise, small holes, and islands keep the boundary granular. ADDITIONAL CUSTOM BLENDS

Straight seams and shaped masks.

Cat + dog. A similarity transform aligns the eye centers and nose, then a vertical mask joins the pale backgrounds and central white markings.

Earth + Moon. Estimated circle centers and radii bring both bodies to a shared 300-pixel radius before the vertical blend.

Lily + firework. An HSV-derived petal mask extracts the entire bloom. A 400-pixel, gold-orange flower, wider feathered edge, and subtle central glow help it merge with the radiating trails.

BELLS & WHISTLES · COLOR

All final blends operate per RGB channel. Color is especially useful in the Oraple, Earth/Moon, sand, and lily experiments: coarse color transitions are smoothed by the mask stack while fine chromatic texture remains attached to its source. For the hybrid images, grayscale avoids conflicting color fields and makes the near/far frequency change easier to judge.

MOST IMPORTANT TAKEAWAY

Filtering is not merely “making an image blurry or sharp.” It decides which structures survive at a particular viewing scale.

The same Gaussian appears throughout the project, but its role changes: it suppresses noise before edge detection, defines the effective cutoff of a hybrid image, separates Laplacian bands, and turns a hard binary mask into a frequency-dependent seam. The most important practical lesson was that alignment and parameter choice matter as much as the equation. A mathematically valid blend can still fail perceptually when silhouettes, landmarks, colors, or frequency weights disagree.
